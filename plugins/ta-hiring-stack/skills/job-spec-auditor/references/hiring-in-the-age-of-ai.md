## 1. The Shift

Something fundamental changed over winter 2025. Over the Christmas break, while most hiring teams were planning pipelines for the year ahead, the world of work quietly crossed a threshold.

Andrej Karpathy (formerly Tesla, OpenAI) described the moment precisely. In November 2024, his split was 80% manual coding and 20% AI-assisted. By December, it had flipped completely: 80% agents, 20% human edits.

> "I went from 80% manual coding and 20% agents in November to 80% agent coding and 20% manual edits by December. I really am mostly programming in English now, the power to operate over software in large code actions is just too net useful."
> — **Andrej Karpathy**, formerly Tesla, OpenAI

Boris Cherny, who leads the Claude Code team at Anthropic, went further:

> "Pretty much 100% of our code is written by Claude Code. I shipped 22 PRs yesterday and 27 the day before, each one 100% written by Claude."
> — **Boris Cherny**, Head of Claude Code, Anthropic

In design, the shift is equally sharp:

> "That design process we were taught, where you diverge, converge, diverge, converge, that's basically dead. You as a designer actually do not have the time to make these beautiful mocks anymore. We used to go off and make a two-year, five-year vision. Now it becomes a vision that's three to six months out, sometimes just a prototype that points people in the right direction."
> — **Jenny Wen**, Head of Design, Claude Cowork at Anthropic, formerly Figma, Dropbox

The ratio of design time spent on mocking and prototyping has shifted from 60–70% of design work to 30–40%.

In go-to-market, the shift is from following playbooks to building and iterating them. The most valuable GTM people in 2026 aren't those with the deepest Salesforce knowledge, they're the ones who can design the system, read the data, and redesign when the data tells them to.

For talent leaders, the implication is sharp: the proxy signals you've used to identify quality — years of experience, degree pedigree, tool expertise, process adherence — are increasingly poor predictors of performance. The bar has moved. This guide helps you reset it.

## 3. Old signals vs new signals, by function

### Engineering

Old signals, losing predictive value:

- Years of experience in a specific language or framework ("5+ years Python required")
- Computer Science degree as a proxy for engineering quality
- Deep specialisation in a single stack as a positive signal
- Algorithm and data structure mastery as a core hiring bar
- Speed of hand-written code as a performance indicator

New signals, what actually predicts performance:

- Ships end-to-end independently, owns a feature from problem to production
- Generalist mindset, has worked across the stack or credibly shows they can
- First-principles thinker, frames the right problem before writing a line of code
- AI-native workflow, uses AI tools as a default, not a novelty
- Production ownership, monitors, iterates, and stays accountable post-ship

### GTM & Sales

Old signals, losing predictive value:

- Proven Rolodex in a sector or "strong existing network"
- Years in a specific vertical as the headline requirement
- Deep expertise in Salesforce / HubSpot required
- Business or Marketing degree required
- Track record of closing £Xm in ARR as the headline bar
- Playbook adherence framed as reliability

New signals, what actually predicts performance:

- Builds the system, not just runs it — sees gaps in the revenue motion and redesigns around them
- AI-native execution — uses AI for prospecting, research, outreach, and workflow automation, not occasionally, as standard
- Data literate — reads their own metrics, spots their own problems, doesn't wait for an analyst
- T-shaped — deep in their core GTM function, genuinely fluent in the others, understands the full funnel
- Adaptable by track record — can demonstrate how their approach has changed; what they did 18 months ago is different from what they do now

### Design

Old signals, losing predictive value:

- Polished high-fidelity portfolio as the primary signal
- Deep Figma mastery as a headline requirement
- "Trust the process" design methodology
- Long-horizon vision work (two-year, five-year vision decks)
- Design as a quality gate ("no feature ships without design review")

New signals, what actually predicts performance:

- Taste and judgment — has opinions and can defend them; recognises when something isn't right and articulates why
- Works at engineering pace — gives directional feedback in real time, doesn't gate momentum
- Prototype-first — 3–6 month horizons, not 5-year decks
- Embedded with engineering, not upstream of it
- Comfortable with ambiguity; AI collaboration as a design instrument

### Product

Old signals, losing predictive value:

- PRD-writing skill as the primary craft
- MBA pedigree
- Deep frameworks fluency (RICE / ICE / MoSCoW) as a defensible signal
- Requirements-management process discipline
- Certifications (Pragmatic / CSPO)

New signals, what actually predicts performance:

- Decision-maker on what gets built — has a view and defends it
- Customer-discovery rigour over PRD craft — discovery in days, not quarters
- AI-native for synthesis and prototyping
- Engineering-pace shipping — doesn't slow the team down with process it doesn't need
- T-shaped: engineering + design + GTM fluent

### Data & Analytics

Old signals, losing predictive value:

- Specific tool fluency (Looker / Tableau / Power BI) as the headline signal
- SQL years-of-experience
- Statistics qualifications without applied evidence
- Report-production volume
- "PhD preferred" with no clear research need

New signals, what actually predicts performance:

- Acts on data signals without an analyst layer
- Builds systems and feedback loops, not just reports
- AI-native — LLMs integrated with ATS / CRM / pipeline tooling
- Influences strategy, doesn't just describe history
- Systems thinker about the whole data architecture

### Cross-functional (all functions, and roles outside the five)

Old signals: years of experience as the primary filter, degree pedigree, "we're a fast-paced, hard-working team" language, tool-specific certifications.

New signals — the four cross-cutting competencies: AI fluency, autonomy & decision-making, adaptability, cross-functional fluency.

## 4. The skills matrix

What each competency looks like at junior vs senior level. Use this to calibrate the Seniority Calibration lens: does the spec describe the right bar for the level being hired?

### Cross-functional competencies (all functions)

**AI Fluency & Adoption**

- Junior / mid: Uses AI tools daily. Has changed at least one core workflow as a result. Can describe specifically how.
- Senior, what great looks like: Designs AI-powered systems and workflows. Multiplies team output, not just their own. Teaches others and stays ahead of the tooling curve.

**Autonomy & Decision-Making**

- Junior / mid: Unblocks themselves when stuck. Tries multiple approaches before escalating. Makes sensible decisions within their scope.
- Senior: Sets direction with minimal guidance. Makes the call in ambiguous situations and moves. Rare escalations, and when they do escalate, they come with a proposed solution.

**Adaptability**

- Junior / mid: Adopts new tools and approaches quickly. Coachable. Can name something they've changed their mind about in the last year.
- Senior: Redesigns their own playbook when the environment changes. Doesn't wait to be told. Demonstrates a pattern of evolution — what they do today is meaningfully different from 18 months ago.

**Cross-functional Fluency**

- Junior / mid: Communicates clearly across teams. Understands adjacent functions at a basic level. Shows genuine curiosity about the wider business.
- Senior: Drives joint initiatives across functions. Understands the full system, not just their lane. Builds alignment without being asked to.

### Engineering competencies

**Technical Craft**

- Junior / mid: Writes clean, reviewable code with AI assistance. Understands what they've shipped well enough to own it. Ships features end-to-end.
- Senior: Makes system-level architectural decisions. Owns a complete product area. High code quality even when shipping fast. Can review and improve AI-generated output critically.

**Generalist Mindset**

- Junior / mid: Has worked across at least two areas of the stack (front-end / back-end / infra). Curious about unfamiliar territory. Not defensive about working outside comfort zone.
- Senior: Designs and ships across the full stack. Comfortable as the only engineer on a project. Runs small-team output (2–4 people's worth) as a solo practitioner with AI tools.

**Production Ownership**

- Junior / mid: Monitors their shipped features. Responds quickly to issues. Doesn't consider something "done" the moment it merges.
- Senior: Stays accountable for outcomes, not just code. Learns from production incidents, implements systemic fixes. Sets up monitoring proactively, before being asked.

**Problem Framing**

- Junior / mid: Thinks about the problem before writing code. Asks clarifying questions. Can identify when a brief is unclear and pushes back constructively.
- Senior: Consistently identifies the right problem to solve, not just the stated one. Shapes technical direction through better framing. Prevents wasted effort upstream.

### GTM & Sales competencies

**Data Literacy**

- Junior / mid: Reads their own pipeline, conversion, and performance metrics. Can spot a problem in the numbers and describe what's causing it.
- Senior: Builds reporting systems. Designs dashboards and feedback loops. Acts on data signals without needing an analyst to interpret them first.

**Systems Thinking**

- Junior / mid: Understands the full revenue funnel beyond their role. Can describe how their work affects upstream and downstream performance.
- Senior: Designs the revenue architecture. Spots and fixes systemic gaps. Thinks like a product manager about the GTM system itself, not just their own numbers.

**Playbook Building**

- Junior / mid: Follows existing frameworks but actively looks for improvements. Can document what's working and why. Proposes iterations.
- Senior: Builds playbooks from scratch, including for new motions or markets. Tests, measures, and iterates systematically. Leaves the next person better off.

**AI-Native Execution**

- Junior / mid: Uses AI tools for research, outreach drafting, and content. Can describe which tools they use and why.
- Senior: Automates significant portions of the GTM workflow. Designs AI-assisted sequences, scoring systems, and prospecting pipelines. Reduces time-on-task by a meaningful multiple.

### Design competencies

**Taste & Judgment**

- Junior / mid: Strong aesthetic sensibility. Has opinions and can defend them. Recognises when something isn't right and articulates why, not just that.
- Senior: Makes the call on what gets built and why. Balances aesthetic quality with speed and user value. Sets the standard others design towards.

## 5. Rewrite the JD

Most job descriptions are still written for a world that no longer exists. What to cut, what to add, and the language that signals the right thing to the right candidates, by function.

### Engineering

Cut from your JD:

- "5+ years of experience in [language or framework]"
- "Computer Science degree or equivalent"
- "Deep expertise in [specific technology]"
- "Strong algorithms and data structures knowledge"
- "Experience with enterprise-scale engineering organisations"

Add to your JD:

- "You ship end-to-end. You own a feature from problem definition to production, with AI as a core tool in your workflow."
- "Generalist by instinct. You've worked across the stack or shown you can. Depth in one area, genuine curiosity about all of them."
- "First-principles thinker. You frame the right problem before writing a line of code, and you push back when the brief is wrong."
- "AI-native. You don't use AI tools occasionally. They're built into how you work. You can describe exactly how."
- "Production ownership. You don't consider something done when it merges. You monitor, iterate, and stay accountable."

### GTM & Sales

Cut from your JD:

- "X+ years in SaaS / [vertical] sales"
- "Proven Rolodex in [sector]" or "strong existing network"
- "Deep expertise in Salesforce / HubSpot required"
- "Business or Marketing degree required"
- "Track record of closing £Xm in ARR" as the headline bar

Add to your JD:

- "You build the system, not just run it. You see gaps in the revenue motion and redesign around them."
- "AI-native execution. You use AI for prospecting, research, outreach, and workflow automation. Not occasionally, as standard."
- "Data literate. You read your own metrics, spot your own problems, and don't wait for an analyst to tell you what the numbers mean."
- "T-shaped. Deep in your core GTM function, genuinely fluent in the others. You understand the full funnel."
- "Adaptable by track record. You can demonstrate how your approach has changed. What you did 18 months ago is different from what you do now."

Copy-ready language for your JD:

> We're looking for GTM people who build the system, not just work within it. You'll be expected to read your own data, design your own playbooks, and use AI tools as a natural part of how you operate. If you've got strong opinions about how revenue teams should work and evidence your views have evolved, let's talk.

### Design

Cut from your JD:

- "Expert in Figma, must demonstrate deep proficiency"
- "Portfolio showing high-fidelity polished work"
- "Strong UX research background, comfortable leading full discovery sprints"
- "Trust the process" methodology language
- Design-as-gate language ("no feature ships without design review")

Add to your JD:

- "You have taste and judgment, and you defend your calls. You don't present three options and wait for the room to decide."
- "You work at engineering pace. You give directional feedback in real time. You don't gate engineering, you enable it."
- "You use AI as a design instrument. You direct it to test directions quickly and cheaply before investing in refinement."
- "Prototype-first. A rough prototype in a day beats two weeks of high-fidelity work when the direction is still open."

## 6. Interview guide

Questions designed to surface candidates who've genuinely adapted to the AI shift, not those who just know the right vocabulary. Each includes what it surfaces and the green / red flags to listen for.

### AI Mindset (all functions)

**"Walk me through how your day-to-day work has actually changed in the last 12 months because of AI. What do you do now that you didn't before, and what have you stopped doing entirely?"**

What this surfaces: genuine adoption vs surface-level awareness. The specificity of their answer tells you everything.

- Green flags: describes specific workflow changes, not just tools they've tried; can name something they've stopped doing (genuine displacement, not just addition); shows judgment about when AI helps and when it doesn't.
- Red flags: "I use ChatGPT for emails sometimes" (no workflow change, surface-level); talks about AI tools in the abstract, can't describe their own usage specifically; nothing has changed — they're aware but haven't adapted.

**"If you had 10 times more access to AI tools than you have now, what would you do differently in your role? Be specific."**

What this surfaces: imagination, ambition, and whether the candidate has genuinely thought about what AI unlocks at scale. Vague answers reveal surface-level engagement. Specific answers reveal someone who thinks in systems.

- Green flags: specific and ambitious — describes a clear change in how they'd operate, not just "I'd be more efficient"; shows they've thought about current constraints and what removing them would enable; talks about team-level or company-level impact, not just personal productivity.
- Red flags: vague ("I'd probably be faster at things"); defensive ("I'm not sure more tools would actually help" without reasoning); describes the same things they already do, no step-change thinking.

### Autonomy (all functions)

**"Tell me about a time you were completely blocked and there was no one senior to help you. What did you do, and how did it resolve?"**

What this surfaces: self-direction, resourcefulness, and willingness to make calls under uncertainty.

- Green flags: describes what they tried first, second, and third before escalating; made a judgment call and moved, then iterated based on outcome; comfortable that the decision might not have been perfect, owns it anyway.
- Red flags: "I raised a blocker in stand-up and waited" (passive); first action was escalation, not exploration; can't recall a specific example.

### Technical Craft (Engineering)

**"You've just shipped a feature built substantially with AI assistance. The code works, but you're not fully confident you understand every part of it. What do you do?"**

What this surfaces: production ownership, intellectual honesty, and how candidates balance speed with accountability. There's no single right answer, but you'll learn a lot from how they reason about it.

- Green flags: reviews the output critically, understands enough to own what's shipped; sets up monitoring, knows how to detect problems quickly; describes a threshold — how much understanding is "enough" and why.
- Red flags: "if it works, it works" (no ownership instinct); "I would never ship code I don't fully understand" (misses the practical reality of AI-assisted development); no mention of monitoring, observability, or what happens after merge.

### Taste & Judgment (Design)

**"An engineer has used AI to build a feature overnight that would have taken two weeks before. It works, but the UX is rough. How do you approach this?"**

What this surfaces: whether the designer is an enabler or a gatekeeper. Whether they can give useful feedback fast, without stopping momentum. Adapted from Jenny Wen's interview philosophy at Anthropic.

- Green flags: gives specific, fast guidance; distinguishes "rough but right direction" from "rough and wrong direction"; helps them iterate without slowing them down — praises the pace, redirects the execution; asks "what problem is this solving?" before applying aesthetic judgment.
- Red flags: "it needs to go through design review before it ships" (gatekeeper reflex); "I'd need to create proper specs first" (process-first, blocks momentum); focuses only on aesthetic quality, not on whether the direction is right.

### Systems Thinking (GTM & Sales)

**"Tell me about a gap you spotted in your company's revenue process that wasn't technically your responsibility. What did you do about it?"**

What this surfaces: T-shaped thinking, cross-functional ownership, and whether candidates understand the system they operate within, not just their own lane.

- Green flags: describes understanding the full funnel, not just their part; took action or escalated with a proposed solution, not just a complaint; can describe the outcome, even if imperfect.
- Red flags: "that wasn't my area"; flagged it once in a meeting and moved on, no follow-through; can't describe the wider revenue system they worked within.

### Adaptability (all functions)

Listen for candidates who show what they replaced an old approach with — the evolution should be complete, not just acknowledged. Red flag: a very minor change described as a major evolution (lack of proportion).

## 7. Candidate scorecard

Rate the candidate 1–5 on each dimension:

1. **AI Fluency & Adoption** — has genuinely changed how they work because of AI: specific, evidenced, not surface-level
2. **Autonomy & Decision-Making** — unblocks themselves, makes calls under uncertainty, rarely needs explicit permission to move
3. **Adaptability** — track record of evolving: what they do today is different from 18 months ago, by design
4. **Role-specific Craft** — the core technical or functional skill for the role: evidenced, not claimed
5. **Cross-functional Thinking** — understands the wider system, takes ownership beyond their immediate lane
