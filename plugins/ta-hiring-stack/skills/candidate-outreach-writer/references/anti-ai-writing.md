# Anti-AI Writing Rules — Candidate Outreach Edition

This document specifies the writing rules the Talent Persuasion Framework applies to every generated message. The framework reads this as part of its knowledge base before generating any outreach. AI-generated outreach has telltale signs that passive candidates have learned to filter out instantly. The rules below replace them with specific, direct writing that reads as a thoughtful internal recruiter or hiring manager.

## Core principle

Every sentence must earn its place with something specific to this role, this company, this candidate, or this moment. If a sentence could appear in any recruiter email from any company about any role, cut it.

## Positive rules

- Be direct. Use "is" and "are." Not "represents an opportunity to," not "serves as," not "marks a critical hire."
- Use specific numbers and named facts. "Around 12,000 customers, growing 30 percent year on year." Not "fast-growing scale-up."
- Vary sentence length deliberately. Short for emphasis. Longer when an idea needs unpacking. Never as a stylistic tic.
- Let one idea breathe before moving to the next. Avoid stacking three claims into one sentence.
- Close on a single specific ask ("worth a 15-minute look?"), not a summary of the role.
- Ground every claim in something concrete: a named manager, a specific product surface, a named customer cohort.
- Use the same word twice if it is the right word. Synonym rotation reads as automated.

## Recruiter clichés to ban completely

- "I came across your profile," "I noticed you worked at [Company] for [years]"
- "Exciting opportunity," "game-changing role," "rocket ship," "disruptive," "cutting-edge"
- "Passionate team," "world-class team," "A-players," "rockstar," "ninja," "guru," "wizard"
- "Wear many hats," "hungry self-starter," "make an impact," "are you the one?"
- "Hope you are doing well," "just touching base," "just circling back," "I'll keep this brief"
- "I'd love to learn more about you" used as an opener

## Generic AI-tell vocabulary to ban

- "Delve," "underscore," "vibrant," "tapestry," "interplay," "intricate," "garner," "pivotal," "showcase," "foster," "align with"
- "Leverages," "encompasses," "facilitates," "utilized," "commenced," "subsequent to," "prior to," "in order to"
- "Stands as," "serves as," "is a testament to," "plays a vital / crucial / pivotal role"
- "It is important to note," "it is worth noting," "moreover," "furthermore," "in addition" (especially back-to-back)
- "Setting the stage for," "marking a shift," "evolving landscape," "reflects broader trends"

## Recruitment-pitch structures to avoid

- The company history dump: "Founded in 2015, [Company] has grown from a small team into a global force..."
- The benefits salad: "We offer competitive salary, generous benefits, and a collaborative culture"
- The responsibilities list dressed as outreach: "The role involves managing X, building Y, and collaborating with Z"
- The vague hook: "I think you would be a great fit for an exciting role we have"
- The double-CTA: "Reply if interested, or click here, or apply directly"
- Email salutations and sign-offs as inline body text ("To your success")

## Contrast constructions: specifics only

Move's signature move is "Most X are Y. This one is Z." This works ONLY when X and Y and Z are all concrete and specific. Example: "Most Staff Engineer roles at this stage optimise systems someone else designed. This one is greenfield architecture for a $120bn vertical that has never been digitised."

Vague reframes are banned: "This is not just about writing code, it is about shaping the future." Also banned in all forms: "Not just X, but Y," "Not only X, but also Y," "But rather."

## Punctuation, structure, tone

- No em dashes. No en dashes. Use commas, full stops, colons, or parentheses.
- No rule-of-three groupings ("ambitious, smart, hungry"). Specific or nothing.
- No paragraph ending with "in summary," "in conclusion," or "overall" followed by a restatement.
- No vague attributions ("industry experts say"). Cite a named source or skip the claim.
- No "likely," "appears to," "seems to," "tends to" when a direct statement is possible.
- No knowledge disclaimers ("as of [date]," "based on what I know").

## Quick audit checklist

Before finalising any generated sequence, the framework verifies:

- Does every sentence contain something specific to this role, company, candidate, or moment?
- Is "is" or "are" used where "serves as" or "represents" might have crept in?
- Any words from the banned vocabulary list? Any recruiter clichés?
- Does the closing land on a single specific ask, or does it summarise?
- Are contrast constructions concrete on both sides, or vague filler?
- Any em-dashes or en-dashes anywhere in the writing?
- Could any sentence be deleted without losing meaning?
