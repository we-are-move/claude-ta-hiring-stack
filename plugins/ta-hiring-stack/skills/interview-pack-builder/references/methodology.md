## Part 1: The framework

Move's documented methodology for turning hiring manager intake meetings into structured hiring packs that raise the evaluation bar. Built across 60+ talent partnerships and 1,000+ hires in engineering, GTM, design, and senior specialist roles.

Most interview processes fail quietly. The hiring manager describes the role once, every interviewer improvises from memory, questions lead the candidate to the desired answer, and the debrief runs on impressions. Strong candidates get screened out by proxy requirements they never had a chance to disprove, and weak candidates get through because nobody defined what a strong answer sounded like before the interview started. The framework codifies the alternative: one structured intake feeding one confirmed set of requirements, from which every question, answer guideline, assessment, and scorecard is generated and traceable.

### Why the bar has moved

The proxy signals most hiring processes still screen on (years of experience, degree pedigree, named-tool tenure, single-stack depth) are losing predictive value fast. The numbers behind the shift:

- 92 percent of hiring managers say soft skills now outrank hard skills, up sharply since 2024.
- Skills-based assessments are 5 times more predictive of performance than education or credentials.
- 45 percent of companies have dropped degree requirements for key roles in the last 12 months.
- 78 percent of hiring managers say they have made a bad hire by prioritising hard skills over mindset.
- Companies using skills-based hiring report 98 percent higher retention of top performers.

At the same time, AI has changed what the work itself looks like. Engineers increasingly direct and review AI-generated code rather than hand-writing it. GTM people design and iterate the playbook rather than executing one. Designers trade polished mocks for fast, directional prototypes. An interview process that still tests hand-craft speed, tool tenure, and process adherence is testing the previous version of the job.

### The golden thread

The framework's structural rule: every element of the hiring pack traces back to a confirmed requirement, and every must-have requirement is tested somewhere in the loop, exactly once at full depth.

- The intake transcript and job spec are codified into requirements: categorised, split into must-have and nice-to-have, with explicit disqualifiers.
- The codification is confirmed by the user (and played back to the hiring manager for sign-off via the briefing) before anything else is generated.
- Every interview question maps to one requirement. Every answer guideline defines strong and weak for that requirement. Every assessment tests requirements the conversation stages cannot.
- The scorecard aggregates the same dimensions, scored independently before any debrief discussion.

When a hiring decision is challenged (internally or legally), this thread is the defence: here is what we agreed we needed, here is how we tested it, here is the evidence we scored.

### Requirements codification

Requirements are grouped into categories drawn from the hiring manager's own language (Technical Skills, Traits, Leadership, Domain Knowledge, or whatever the intake surfaces), then split into must-have and nice-to-have. Two disciplines apply:

- **Evidence statements, not labels.** "Senior" is a label. "Has owned a production system end-to-end through at least one major scaling event" is evidence. Codify the evidence.
- **Use only what was discussed.** The codification contains what the hiring manager said and what the job spec states. Gaps are flagged, never filled with plausible inventions.

### The AI-era audit

Before the codification is confirmed, every requirement is audited against the question: is this evidence of capability, or a proxy for it? Flagged proxies get a proposed reframing. Typical conversions:

| Proxy requirement (flag) | Evidence-based reframing |
| --- | --- |
| 5+ years of [language or framework] | Ships production features end-to-end and can critically review AI-generated code in a language the team uses |
| Computer Science degree or equivalent | Demonstrated excellence: something real they built, shipped, and can walk through under questioning |
| Deep expertise in [single tool] | Learns and exploits new tooling fast; can describe a workflow they rebuilt in the last year |
| Must have [industry] experience | Can demonstrate transferable pattern recognition; ramp risk assessed in the case assessment, not screened at CV |
| Proven network or Rolodex | Builds pipeline systematically from cold; can show the system, not the contact list |

If the hiring manager never raised AI fluency, autonomy, or adaptability, the framework proposes them as additions, clearly marked as Move-recommended rather than silently mixed into what was said. The hiring manager accepts or rejects them at sign-off. If they are not thinking about how the bar has moved, this is the moment they start.

### What the new bar looks like, by function

#### Engineering

- **Ships end-to-end:** owns a feature from problem definition to production, with AI as a core tool in the workflow.
- **Generalist range:** has worked across the stack by choice; comfortable as the only engineer on a problem.
- **First-principles problem framing:** identifies the right problem before writing a line of code.
- **Production ownership:** monitors, iterates, and stays accountable after merge.

Red flags: single-language identity, AI resistance at the expense of output, hands off at merge, cannot unblock themselves.

#### GTM and Sales

- **System builder:** designs and iterates the playbook, does not just execute one.
- **Data literate:** reads and acts on their own metrics without an analyst.
- **T-shaped:** deep in one GTM function, genuinely fluent in the others; understands the full funnel.
- **AI-native execution:** research, prospecting, and workflow automation as standard practice.

Red flags: rigid playbook dependency, siloed thinking, no data muscle, AI scepticism without curiosity.

#### Design

- **Taste and judgment:** makes the call on what gets built and why, not just what it looks like.
- **Works at engineering pace:** ships directional design fast, enables rather than gates.
- **Prototype-first, short-horizon vision:** a rough prototype in a day over a polished deck in two weeks.
- **Decision accountability:** owns what actually gets built.

Red flags: process attachment, gatekeeper reflex, no opinion, mock-first instinct.

### Cross-cutting dimensions (every role)

Four dimensions belong on every scorecard regardless of function, each calibrated junior versus senior:

| Dimension | Junior / mid level | Senior: what great looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI fluency and adoption | Uses AI tools daily; has changed at least one core workflow and can describe specifically how | Designs AI-powered workflows that multiply team output; teaches others; stays ahead of the tooling curve |
| Autonomy and decision-making | Unblocks themselves; tries multiple approaches before escalating | Sets direction with minimal guidance; makes the call in ambiguity; escalates rarely and with a proposed solution |
| Adaptability | Adopts new approaches quickly; can name something they changed their mind about in the last year | Redesigns their own playbook when the environment changes, without being told |
| Cross-functional fluency | Communicates clearly across teams; curious about adjacent functions | Drives joint initiatives; understands the full system, not just their lane |

### Non-leading question construction

The full rules live in Question_Construction_Rules.docx and every generated question is audited against them. The core discipline: the candidate must never be able to reverse-engineer the desired answer from the question. The requirement is never restated; technologies and domains appear only in the answer guidelines; traits are tested through situations and outcomes, not self-assessment. Each question comes with a follow-up that deepens (never rescues), a strong answer guideline, and a weak answer guideline, all observable and specific.

### The screener

The Talent Partner's qualification call runs a fixed architecture: five reusable opening questions to orient and warm up, a scorecard of 4 to 6 question bank entries chosen for screening leverage (including 1 to 2 values or trait-based), an admin checklist captured rather than interrogated (notice period, salary expectations, location and right to work, office expectations, competing processes), and soft signals recorded but never asked: quality of communication, preparedness, the questions the candidate asked, ability to explain without jargon.

### Process design and assessments

The interview process table assigns each stage explicit ownership of specific requirements, so nothing is double-tested and nothing is untested. Stages and depth are tailored to seniority, and divergent paths (for example, no system design round for mid-level) are stated in the table rather than improvised later.

Assessment design follows four rules:

- **Mirror the job.** The exercise should be the closest legal and practical approximation of the actual work. Real backlog bugs over puzzle algorithms; the team's actual funnel data over invented cases.
- **Cap the time.** Take-homes default to a 2 hour maximum unless the company's evaluation philosophy explicitly allows more. Candidate time is a cost the process spends; spend it like money.
- **Let AI in, deliberately.** Where the philosophy supports it, at least one assessment should permit or expect AI tool use and evaluate how the candidate directs, reviews, and owns the output. Banning the tools the job runs on tests a job that no longer exists.
- **Score against a rubric.** Every assessment ships with observable criteria written before the candidate starts. Impressions are recorded as soft signals, never as scores.

### Scoring and debrief

The scorecard carries the cross-cutting dimensions plus role-specific craft, each with a one-line definition of a top score, on the company's chosen scale (default 1 to 5). The default debrief rule: interviewers score independently before any discussion, then debrief. Calibration discussion changes future rubrics, not submitted scores.

## Part 2: Reference pack

A compressed, fully anonymised reference showing what the framework generates. The company, industry, and details below have been altered. Use this as grounding for structure and quality, not as a template for specifics.

**Role:** Staff Software Engineer at a Series B B2B commerce platform digitising a previously offline industry vertical. Dublin, hybrid. Reports to the CTO. The intake flagged greenfield architecture ownership, a small squad to direct, and a culture of shipping in days.

### Requirements codification (excerpt)

| Category | Must have | Nice to have |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical craft | Has architected and owned a production system end-to-end through a scaling event; critically reviews AI-generated code before shipping | Marketplace or commerce domain exposure |
| Ways of working | Ships in days without sacrificing system quality; AI-native workflow, specific and evidenced | Experience directing a small squad |
| Traits | Autonomy: unblocks themselves and makes calls in ambiguity; adaptability with a track record | Comfort with non-uniform, messy problem domains |

**Disqualifiers:** needs a fully formed spec before starting; AI resistance at the expense of output; hands off at merge.

**AI-era audit:** the intake asked for "8+ years of backend experience". Flagged as a proxy; reframed (and accepted) as the architecture-ownership evidence statement above. AI fluency was not raised by the hiring manager; proposed as an addition and accepted.

### Question bank (three sample entries)

#### 1. Architecture ownership

"Walk me through the most consequential technical decision you have made in the last two years. What were the options, what did you choose, and what happened?"

- **Follow-up:** "What would you decide differently today, and what changed your view?"
- **Strong answer:** Frames the decision in terms of constraints and trade-offs; owns a consequence that was not perfect; the system survived growth. References to specific scaling pressures, data model choices, or service boundaries.
- **Weak answer:** Describes a team decision they were near rather than one they owned; no consequences either way; cannot say what they would change.

#### 2. AI fluency (note: the question never names a tool)

"Walk me through how your day-to-day work has actually changed in the last 12 months. What do you do now that you did not do before, and what have you stopped doing entirely?"

- **Follow-up:** "Where does that approach fail you? When do you not use it?"
- **Strong answer:** Specific workflow changes with AI tooling, including something genuinely displaced, not just added. Shows judgment about where the tools help and where they do not.
- **Weak answer:** "I use ChatGPT for emails sometimes." Tools in the abstract; no displacement; nothing has structurally changed.

#### 3. Autonomy

"Tell me about a time you were completely blocked and there was no one senior to help you. What did you do, and how did it resolve?"

- **Follow-up:** "What did you try first, second, and third before deciding?"
- **Strong answer:** Multiple approaches tried before escalating; made a judgment call and moved; owns the imperfect outcome.
- **Weak answer:** "I raised it in stand-up and waited." First action was escalation; cannot recall a specific example.

### Interview process

| Stage | Type | Details | Participants |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TA screen | Structured call | 30 min: openers, 5 scorecard questions, admin checklist | Talent Partner |
| Technical deep dive | Live working session | 90 min on a real backlog problem, AI tools on, rubric-scored | CTO + senior engineer |
| Architecture review | Whiteboard / discussion | 60 min: candidate walks through a system they owned; panel probes decisions | CTO + peer |
| Values + close | Conversation | 45 min: traits scorecard questions, candidate questions, comp expectations | Co-founder |

### Assessment (one of two)

- **Exercise:** Live working session on a real, recently closed backlog problem (sanitised). AI tools explicitly permitted and expected. The candidate frames the problem, directs their tools, and ships a reviewed solution.
- **Tests:** Technical craft, AI fluency, problem framing, working style under collaboration.
- **Time cap:** 90 minutes, hard stop.

**Scoring rubric:**

- Framed the problem before touching the solution; asked questions that changed the approach.
- Directed AI tools effectively: prompts, iteration, and critical review of output, not blind acceptance.
- The shipped result is production-plausible: handles the edge case planted in the exercise.
- Explained decisions in plain language a non-engineer on the panel could follow.

### Scorecard

Scale 1 to 5. Independent scoring before discussion.

| Dimension | What a top score looks like |
| --- | --- |
| AI fluency and adoption | Specific, evidenced workflow change; directs and critically reviews AI output as a default |
| Autonomy and decision-making | Unblocks themselves; makes calls under uncertainty; escalates with proposed solutions |
| Adaptability | What they do today is meaningfully different from 18 months ago, by design |
| Technical craft (role-specific) | Architecture-level decisions evidenced through a real owned system; quality at speed |
| Cross-functional fluency | Explains technical decisions in business terms; collaborates beyond the engineering lane |
