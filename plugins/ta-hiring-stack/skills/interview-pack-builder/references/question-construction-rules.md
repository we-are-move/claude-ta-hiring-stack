# Question Construction Rules — The Hiring Bar Framework

Move, 2026

## Why non-leading matters

A leading question hands the candidate the answer. "We value autonomy here. Tell me about a time you worked autonomously" produces a story about autonomy from every candidate who wants the job, which is all of them. The question has measured the candidate's ability to detect what you want to hear, which correlates with nothing you are hiring for.

A non-leading question hides the requirement and asks about behaviour, decisions, or scenarios. The candidates who genuinely have the capability surface it because it is how they actually operate. The ones who do not cannot fake specificity under follow-up. The interviewer, armed with strong and weak answer guidelines written in advance, knows exactly what they are listening for. Every question the framework generates is audited against the rules in this document before it reaches a pack.

## The six rules

### Rule 1: Never restate the requirement in the question

The candidate must not be able to reverse-engineer the desired answer from the question itself.

- **Bad:** "This role needs someone who can handle ambiguity. How do you handle ambiguity?"
- **Good:** "Tell me about the last project you started where the brief turned out to be wrong. What did you do?"

### Rule 2: Technologies, industries, and domains live in the rubric, not the question

If the requirement is a technology, industry, or domain, do not mention it in the question. Reference it only in the answer guidelines. This stops the candidate keyword-matching and lets genuine depth surface naturally.

- **Bad:** "Tell me about your Kubernetes experience."
- **Good:** "Describe the most complex system you have run in production. What broke most often and what did you do about it?" (Kubernetes, or an equivalent, belongs in the strong answer guideline.)

### Rule 3: Traits and soft skills get situational or outcome-driven prompts

Never ask a candidate to self-assess a trait. Ask for the situation where the trait would have shown up, then listen for it.

- **Bad:** "Would you describe yourself as adaptable?"
- **Good:** "What is something you were confident about 18 months ago that you have since fundamentally changed your view on?"

### Rule 4: Past evidence over hypotheticals, with one exception

"Tell me about a time" beats "What would you do if" because the past actually happened and survives follow-up. The exception: deliberate scenario questions that test live judgment where no clean past example exists, such as: "You have shipped a feature built substantially with AI assistance. It works, but you are not fully confident you understand every part of it. What do you do?" Scenario questions are a scalpel, not the default. At most one or two per pack.

### Rule 5: One question, one target. Follow-ups deepen, never rescue

Each question tests exactly one requirement. The follow-up digs into the same requirement ("What did you try first, second, third?", "What would you do differently today?"). A follow-up that redirects a struggling candidate to an easier version of the question is the interviewer answering for them. Silence after a weak answer is data.

### Rule 6: Nothing legally risky

No questions touching age, family or marital status, pregnancy or parenting plans, health or disability, religion, nationality or origin beyond right-to-work verification, or anything prohibited by the company's compliance answers in the evaluation philosophy. If a hiring manager's intake raised any of these as concerns, the pack addresses the underlying business need legally (e.g. travel requirements stated as requirements, not probed as personal circumstances).

## Banned constructions

- "Would you say you are…" and every other invitation to self-assess.
- "How important is X to you?" (X is clearly important; the candidate knows it.)
- "Tell me about your experience with [named technology]." (Rule 2.)
- "Do you have…" / "Have you ever…" and other yes/no constructions. They collect claims, not evidence.
- Multi-part question stacks. The candidate answers the easiest part and the interviewer rarely returns for the rest.
- Telegraphing preambles: "We really value X here, so…", "This role lives and dies on X…"
- Culture-fit vibes questions with no requirement behind them ("Would you fit in here?", "What is your spirit animal?").
- Brain teasers and puzzle questions untethered from the actual work.

## Answer guideline standards

Every question ships with a strong answer guideline and a weak answer guideline. They are the interviewer's calibration, written before the interview, and they carry most of the pack's value. Standards:

- **Observable and specific.** "Communicates well" is banned. "Explains the technical decision in terms a non-engineer could follow, without being asked to simplify" is a guideline.
- **Specific to the requirement.** A strong answer guideline that could sit under any question is a failed guideline.
- **Calibrated to seniority.** What is strong for a mid-level candidate can be weak for a staff-level one; the guideline states the level it expects.
- **Both directions.** The weak guideline is not the absence of the strong one; it names the actual failure modes, including plausible-sounding ones (the polished answer with no specifics, the team achievement worn as a personal one).
- **Green and red flags where sharper.** For behavioural questions especially, a short list of listen-fors beats prose: what the genuinely capable say, and what the rehearsed say.
- **Past mis-hires inform red flags.** The failure modes from the company's evaluation philosophy (Q10) get woven into weak answer guidelines wherever relevant.

## Audit checklist

Run every question and guideline through this list before it enters a pack:

- The requirement is not restated or paraphrased in the question.
- No technology, industry, or domain name appears in the question (rubric only).
- No self-assessment invitation, yes/no construction, or multi-part stack.
- No telegraphing preamble.
- The question targets exactly one confirmed requirement.
- The follow-up deepens the same target.
- Strong and weak guidelines are observable, specific, and calibrated to the role's seniority.
- Nothing legally risky, and compliance constraints from the philosophy file are respected.
- No em dashes or en dashes anywhere (use commas, colons, parentheses; "3 to 6 months" not "3-6 months").
- Plain language throughout: a busy interviewer can run the question cold.
