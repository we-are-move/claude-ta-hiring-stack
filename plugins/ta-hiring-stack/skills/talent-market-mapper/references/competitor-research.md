# Competitor research: who you're really competing with

The question behind this section: when a strong candidate for this role decides where to work next, which employers are on their list, and how does your company look next to them?

## 1. The three competitor types

**Direct rivals.** Same sector, hiring the same or similar titles right now. Check their live jobs boards, not just their brand.

**Cross-sector poachers.** Different sector, same skills. These are the ones in-house teams miss most often, and they're frequently the most dangerous because they often pay more. Ask: who else needs exactly these skills? Examples of the pattern:
- Backend and data engineers: big tech, banks' digital arms, trading firms, gaming, crypto.
- Credit risk and data science: banks, credit bureaus, insurers, consultancies.
- Enterprise sales: any SaaS or payments company selling to the same buyers.
- Designers and product managers: consumer apps, agencies, big tech.

Include at least two cross-sector employers in every table.

**Local employers.** Who else recruits from the same pool in each location, and what local pay runs at. With a single location, include at least two. With several locations, include at least one per location.

## 2. What to collect for each employer

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Pay signal | A range for this role and level, in local currency. State base salary or total compensation |
| Pay source label | Exactly one of: Published, Crowd-reported, Benchmark, Your data, Not found |
| Versus you | Above, At, Below, or Unknown compared with the person's pay range |
| Publishes pay? | Yes, No, or Partial (some ads only) |
| Flexibility | 1 to 5, plus the policy in a few words ("hybrid, 3 days") |
| Stability and brand | 1 to 5, plus one reason |
| Pull | 1 to 5, plus one reason: how attractive they are to this specific candidate |
| Hiring stance | A few words for the `stance` field: growing, frozen or cutting; recent layoffs, funding, return-to-office mandates, regulatory trouble. Shown under the employer's name in the report |
| Sourcing pool? | True if their current situation makes their people more movable (see section 5) |

The person's own company is the first row, typed "Baseline". Use their supplied data and label it "Your data". Where they didn't supply something, use the public signal and label it accordingly.

## 3. Where to find it

- **Live roles and published pay:** the employer's careers page and its ATS board (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workable, SmartRecruiters and Workday boards are usually public). Many UK and EU ads now publish ranges.
- **Crowd-reported pay:** Levels.fyi (strongest for tech), Glassdoor, Blind. Always label as crowd-reported.
- **Benchmarks:** recruiter salary guides (Hays, Robert Half, Michael Page, Harvey Nash, Morgan McKinley and sector specialists) and published compensation reports. Label as Benchmark.
- **Flexibility:** the employer's careers page, recent job ads, and news coverage of office policies.
- **Stability and stance:** recent news on funding, layoffs, restructures, return-to-office mandates, regulatory fines; Glassdoor rating and recurring review themes.

Note the date you checked each source. Pay and policy change quickly.

**Rules for pay figures:**

- **Open the source.** Never use a figure, policy or event that you've only seen in a search snippet. If the page won't load, treat the fact as not found.
- **Exact role first, stand-in second.** If the employer has no live ad for this role, the same employer's published band for a neighbouring role (for example a senior software engineer band for a senior data engineer) is acceptable, but say so in the competitor note.
- **Base versus total.** Levels.fyi usually reports total compensation; job ads usually show base. Say which one each figure is, and don't compare one against the other as if they were the same.
- **Watch for template figures.** Glassdoor often shows the same national or job-title average on several employers' salary pages. If two employers show an identical figure, it's not company data: use individual submissions or treat it as not found.
- **Not found is a valid answer.** Many employers don't publish pay and have little crowd data for a specific role in a specific city. Write "Not found" and label it so, rather than stretching a weak source.

## 4. Rating rubric

Ratings are qualitative reads of public signals, relative to this market. They are not precise measurements, and the methodology should say so.

**Flexibility**
- 5: fully remote, or remote-first with optional offices
- 4: hybrid, 1 to 2 days a week in office
- 3: hybrid, 3 days
- 2: 4 days, or a recent mandate increasing office time
- 1: fully office-based

**Stability and brand**
- 5: profitable or very well funded, strong reputation in this talent community, Glassdoor 4.2+
- 4: solid funding or revenue, good reputation, no recent turbulence
- 3: mixed signals: average reviews, or some uncertainty
- 2: recent layoffs, restructuring, leadership churn, or poor reviews
- 1: serious instability: repeated cuts, regulatory trouble, or very poor reviews

**Pull** (how strongly this employer attracts *this* candidate profile)
- 5: a destination employer for this skill set; people actively try to get in
- 4: clearly attractive: good work, good pay or strong brand for this profile
- 3: a reasonable option without a standout draw
- 2: weak draw for this profile (wrong problems, low pay, poor reputation for this function)
- 1: actively avoided by this profile

Give the person's own company honest ratings too. A flattering baseline makes the whole report useless.

## 5. Spotting sourcing pools

Some competitors are better treated as places to find candidates than as threats. Flag an employer as a sourcing pool when its people are more likely to move than usual:

- Recent or announced layoffs, restructures or hiring freezes
- A new return-to-office mandate, especially an unpopular one
- Acquisition, merger, or a leadership change affecting the function
- A failed product, lost licence, or regulatory remediation
- Pay or equity falling behind the market (for example, a down round)

Name the event and its date in the talent pools section.

## 6. Common mistakes

- Listing only same-sector rivals. Cross-sector poachers usually set the pay ceiling.
- Treating crowd-reported pay as fact. Always label it.
- Rating the person's own company generously.
- Profiling famous brands that don't actually hire this profile in this location. Check their live roles.
- Naming individuals. Map companies, communities and titles, never specific people.
